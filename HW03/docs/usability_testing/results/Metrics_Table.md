slowest, with no crossover between T1 and T2. Whatever separates these five is stable across two
different tasks, which is what a findability problem looks like and is not what task-specific confusion
would look like.

## Two counts of the same thing, from two sources

The participant-reported counts in the first table were derived from the answer sheets *before* the
paper log was transcribed. Comparing them is free evidence about how much either source can be trusted.

| Hesitations — participant's own account | 1 | 2 | 0 | 2 | 2 | **7** |

**Errors: same total, two cells swapped.** Seven either way. P1 is the one the participant-side count
was already flagged as soft — *"Tôi không đi sai nhiều"*, "not much", not an explicit zero — and the
moderator logged 1 there. P2 described three episodes where the log records two, most likely because
the initial detour to the Events page and the wrong-menu attempt were logged as one. Neither source is
being corrected by the other; the disagreement is two episodes wide on a total of seven.

**Hesitations: 19 against 7, and the direction is the expected one.** The two are not measuring the
same thing and were never going to match. The moderator logged **pauses of three seconds or more** —
including pauses the participant would not remember or would not call hesitation. The participants
reported **moments they were conscious of being uncertain**. That an observer sees roughly 2.7× as many
pauses as the person reports is the ordinary finding in think-aloud work, and it is the reason §6 asks
for an observed count rather than a self-reported one. Had the two matched, that would have been the
suspicious outcome.

## Counting rule for the Err. / Hes. columns

Written down so the numbers can be audited against the five `session_notes/` files, and so the reader
knows exactly what they are not.

- **Error** = an action the participant took that moved them off the success path and had to be undone
  or redone — wrong destination, blocked submit, wrong record opened. One episode = 1, however long it
  lasted. Failing to *guess* something is not an error until it produces a wrong action.
- **Hesitation** = a point of uncertainty the participant reports in their own words (*"dừng lại một
  chút"*, *"phân vân"*, *"không chắc"*, *"hơi lâu"*). **This is not the ≥3 s pause count §6 asks for** —
  that needs a clock on the recording, which was not run. It is a count of self-declared uncertainty
  points, which is a weaker and different thing, and is labelled as such everywhere it appears.
- **Source is the participant, not the moderator.** Both columns are a **lower bound**. An error the
  participant did not notice, or noticed and did not write down, is invisible here. A moderator-observed
  count would be equal or higher, never lower.

| **Both tasks** | 10/10 runs | 2 | 0 | 0 | **7:16 combined** | **7 episodes, 3 of 5 participants** | **7, 4 of 5 participants** |

Mean time is moderator-recorded; the error and hesitation columns here are the participant-reported
figures, with the moderator's own log tabulated in the section above (7 errors, 19 hesitations).
Means are given to one decimal and should be read as descriptive only: at n = 5, "0.8" is a way of
writing "4 across five people", not an estimate of a population rate — and a mean of 5:30 over a
2:58–8:30 range describes nobody in the sample.

**Both tasks completed by all five, so success rate is not where the signal is.** Three of the five
reached the response only after a wrong turn (P2 tried a different menu, P4 opened the wrong request,
P5 tried the bell and fell back to the list), and the SUS spread of 27.5–97.5 says the same thing the
success column cannot: the tasks are *achievable* but the path to them is not *discoverable*. A
success-rate-only reading of this study would conclude the product is fine, which none of the four
open-question answers supports.

**Every one of the seven errors is a navigation or a completeness error; none is a data-loss or a
system error.** Four are "I was in the wrong place" (P2 Events page, P2 wrong menu, P4 several menus,
P4 wrong request), one is the same class on T2 (P5's bell), and two are the form refusing an incomplete
submission — those two are the product working, not failing. Split that way, **5 of 7 errors are
findability and 2 of 7 are successful error prevention**, which is the whole argument of §9 in one line.

**Route taken to the response (T2)** — never steered, so this is a findability result: 4 of 5 used
the requests list, 1 (P3) used the notification bell. P5 tried the bell first and abandoned it.

Nothing was excluded from any aggregate. No run was moderator-intervened, so no time is excluded from
either mean.

## Perceived effort against measured time — the one genuine cross-check in this study

Before the paper log was transcribed, a **rank order of perceived effort** was derived from probe 3
(*"Tốc độ / công sức"*) — each participant's own sentence about how hard the flow felt, with no access
to any clock. Setting it beside the measured times afterwards is the only place in this study where a
figure derived one way can be checked against a figure obtained another way.

| Effort rank (from probe 3) | P | The sentence the rank rests on | T1 | T2 | Errors | SUS |
| :-: | --- | --- | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: |
| 1 | P3 | *"Không có bước nào thừa, việc đính ảnh và gửi diễn ra nhanh"* | 2:58 | 0:22 | 0 | 97.5 |
| 2 | P1 | *"Việc nhập lại khá nhanh, chỉ mất thời gian chọn đúng loại"* | 4:12 | 0:45 | 1 | 80.0 |
| 3 | P5 | *"Không quá chậm, nhưng tôi phải kiểm tra các trường và làm mới trang"* | 5:05 | 1:55 | 1 | 70.0 |
| 4 | P2 | *"Tìm chỗ báo vấn đề và tìm lại yêu cầu hơi lâu"* | 6:45 | 2:10 | 2 | 60.0 |
| 5 | P4 | *"Tốn công nhất là dò nhiều menu và đọc từng yêu cầu để tìm đúng cái mới"* | 8:30 | 3:40 | 3 | 27.5 |

**Five orderings, one sequence, no inversions.** Perceived effort, T1 time, T2 time, error count and SUS
all place the five participants in the order P3 · P1 · P5 · P2 · P4. Two of those five columns are
moderator-recorded on paper during the session; three are participant self-report collected afterwards.
They were produced independently and they agree.

That is what lifts this beyond internal consistency. Self-report columns agreeing with each other proves
little — the same person in the same sitting can be wrong in the same direction across all of them. An
**observed** measure landing in the same order as the self-reported one is a different kind of evidence:
it means the participants' account of how hard the flow felt tracked how long it actually took them.
The SUS spread in §7 is therefore not an artefact of questionnaire mood. It is measuring something real.

**What is still true about the sample.** Five participants, ordinal agreement, no significance test and
none claimed. Five points can order themselves by chance more easily than fifty can, so this is reported
as a consistency check that passed, not as a validated instrument.

## Interpretation note

Five participants surface most of the *findable* problems in a formative, single-user-group study,
but support no statistical claim (no significance, no confidence interval, no benchmark
comparison). Report "3 of 5 participants hit X," give the SUS mean alongside the five individual
scores, and let the severity ranking in the Usability Report carry the argument.
